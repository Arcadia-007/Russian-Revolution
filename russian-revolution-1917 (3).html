Revolutionary Analysis

The Russian Revolution of 1917

Two uprisings in a year ended over 300 years of Romanov rule and put the Bolsheviks in power. What gave the Bolsheviks success. What can we learn about revolutions today?

Central Claim: A revolutionary movement succeeds when it blends three parts: (1) a broadly shared vision of the new order, (2) organized power to seize and keep institutions, and (3) the ability to stop or absorb the state’s force. The Russian Revolution of 1917 met all three. The “Woman, Life, Freedom” protests of 2022 to present have mainly met the first part.

What Counts as Revolutionary Success?

In this analysis a successful revolutionary movement is defined by four criteria taken from history:

  1. Transfer of sovereign power — The old regime loses the power to rule and a new authority takes its place.
  2. Replacement — Key state institutions such as the army, police, bureaucracy and legal system are taken over, neutralised or rebuilt under new control.
  3. Legitimacy or acquiescence — Enough of the people accept or cannot effectively resist the new order.
  4. Durability — The new power structure survives the counter-revolutionary reaction, such as civil war, foreign intervention or internal splits.

The Russian Revolution of 1917 is studied in depth because it is one of the modern cases where a radical party really seized and kept state power. The French Revolution is used as a comparison. These patterns are then applied to the “Woman, Life, Freedom” movement that began after the death of Mahsa (Jina) Amini in September 2022.

Overview of 1917

The Russian Revolution had two uprisings. The February Revolution (March in the Gregorian calendar) overthrew Tsar Nicholas II and ended the Romanov dynasty after more than 300 years. A Provisional Government took power but shared authority unevenly with the Petrograd Soviet of Workers’ and Soldiers’ Deputies. A situation known as “dual power.”

Eight months later the October Revolution (November by the calendar) saw Vladimir Lenin’s Bolsheviks seize control in a coordinated takeover. They created a government based on the soviets, pulled Russia out of World War I and set the stage for the Civil War and the eventual creation of the Soviet Union in 1922.

Note on dates: Russia still used the Julian calendar in 1917. February and October refer to the old-style dates. In the Gregorian calendar the events happened in March and November.

Causes

The revolution did not erupt overnight. Decades of tension, rigid politics and the disaster of World War I combined to make the old order unsustainable.

Autocratic Rule

Nicholas II resisted reform. The Duma created after 1905 had limited power and was repeatedly dissolved when it challenged the throne.

War Exhaustion

Russia suffered losses in World War I. Millions of casualties, collapsing morale and a government unable to supply or lead its armies.

Economic Crisis

Food and fuel shortages hit the cities hard. Inflation soared. Bread rationing in early 1917 triggered panic and mass protests in Petrograd.

Social Inequality

Peasants (the majority) still lacked secure land rights. Urban workers faced harsh conditions, long hours and little political voice.

1905 Revolution

The earlier uprising forced concessions but left core problems unresolved, teaching radicals that the regime could be challenged.

Loss of Legitimacy

Court scandals, military defeats and the perception of a weak out-of-touch monarchy eroded loyalty among elites and the army.

The February Revolution

What began as protests over bread shortages quickly became a full-scale revolt against the monarchy.

On 23 February 1917 (8 March Gregorian) — International Women’s Day — female textile workers in Petrograd marched for bread. Strikes spread quickly. Within days hundreds of thousands of workers were on the streets. When the tsar ordered troops to restore order many soldiers refused to fire and joined the demonstrators instead.

On 2 March (15 March) Nicholas II abdicated. His brother Grand Duke Michael declined the throne unless it was offered by a constituent assembly. More than three centuries of Romanov rule were over.

A Provisional Government was formed, initially led by Prince Georgy Lvov and later by Alexander Kerensky. At the same time the Petrograd Soviet rose as a powerful alternative centre of authority creating dual power.

The February Revolution was largely spontaneous. No single party planned or controlled it. Soldiers’ mutiny proved decisive: once the garrison sided with the crowds the old regime collapsed.

The October Revolution

The Provisional Government struggled from the start. It kept Russia in the war, failed to solve the land question and lost support as economic conditions worsened. Bolshevik influence grew, especially after Lenin returned from exile in April 1917 and issued his April Theses demanding “Peace, Land and Bread” and “All Power to the Soviets.”

Key turning points included the failed July Days uprising, the Kornilov Affair (an attempted coup that the Bolsheviks helped defeat) and the Bolsheviks winning majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets by September.

On the night of 24–25 October 1917 (6–7 November Gregorian) Bolshevik Red Guards and soldiers organised by the Military Revolutionary Committee under Leon Trotsky’s direction seized points in Petrograd. The Winter Palace fell with little bloodshed. Most ministers were arrested; Kerensky fled.

The Second Russian Congress of Soviets endorsed the transfer of power. Lenin’s new government immediately issued the Decree on Peace and the Decree on Land appealing directly to war-weary soldiers and land-hungry peasants.

Key Figures

N

Nicholas II

Last Tsar of Russia

Ruled from 1894 until his abdication in March 1917. His rigid belief in autocracy, poor handling of the war and detachment from popular suffering made him a symbol of a failing system. He and his family were later executed by the Bolsheviks in 1918.

L

Vladimir Lenin

Leader of the Bolsheviks

Returned from exile in April 1917 and pushed for an immediate socialist revolution. His slogans and organisational drive were central to the October seizure of power. He became head of the Soviet government.

T

Leon Trotsky

Organiser of the October uprising

Chairman of the Petrograd Soviet and key architect of the Military Revolutionary Committee. His organisational skill was crucial in the bloodless takeover of the capital.

K

Alexander Kerensky

Head of the Provisional Government

Became the dominant figure after July 1917. He tried to hold the middle ground between conservatives and radicals but lost support by continuing the war and failing to deliver rapid reform.

International Women’s Day marches and strikes begin in Petrograd over bread shortages. Protests escalate rapidly.
27 February – 2 March
Soldiers join the crowds. Nicholas II abdicates. Dual power begins: Provisional Government and Petrograd Soviet.

Lenin returns to Petrograd and presents the April Theses calling for soviet power and an end to the war.

July Days: demonstrations in Petrograd. Bolsheviks are temporarily suppressed; Lenin goes into hiding.
Late August
Kornilov Affair: failed move against the government. Bolsheviks gain credibility by helping defend Petrograd.
September
Bolsheviks win majorities in the Petrograd and Moscow soviets. Trotsky becomes chairman of the Petrograd Soviet.
24–25 October (6–7 November)
October Revolution: Bolsheviks seize points in Petrograd and take the Winter Palace. Power is transferred to the soviets.
26 October onward
Decree on Peace and Decree on Land issued. Council of People’s Commissars formed under Lenin. Civil conflict intensifies.

Comparison with the French Revolution

Both revolutions began with moderate phases that failed to solve core crises, then saw radical minorities take control during moments of dual power or institutional breakdown.

Aspect French Revolution Russian Revolution
Initial trigger Fiscal crisis + Estates-General War exhaustion + food shortages
Early phase Moderate constitutional monarchy Moderate Provisional Government
Radical turn Jacobins / Committee of Public Safety Bolsheviks / Sovnarkom
Leadership Multiple factions; Robespierre central for a time Highly centralized around Lenin & the party
Coercive force National Guard + revolutionary armies Red Guards → Red Army
Outcome after ~10 years Napoleonic dictatorship One-party Soviet state

Key Similarities

Both began with moderate phases that failed. Both saw radical minorities take control during institutional breakdown. Both used emergency measures and violence against internal and external enemies. Both produced authoritarian outcomes rather than stable liberal democracy.

Key Differences

The French Revolution was more ideologically diffuse. The Russian Revolution was driven by a disciplined party with a clear Marxist program. France’s revolution unfolded over a decade of shifting regimes; Russia’s October power transfer was rapid and relatively coordinated.

Primary sources: Lenin’s April Theses (1917) and the Decrees on Peace and Land show the Bolshevik plan of tying real popular demands to the taking of power. The French Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) and Olympe de Gouges’s Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791) show both the promise and the unfinished nature of revolutionary equality claims.

Current Movement: Iran’s “Woman, Life, Freedom”

On 13 September 2022, 22-year-old Mahsa (Jina) Amini, a Kurdish woman from Saqqez, was taken by the morality police in Tehran because she was said to wear her hijab wrongly. She died three days later while in custody. Her death sparked protests across the country that soon took up the slogan “Woman, Life, Freedom” (Jin, Jiyan, Azadi).

Background

The Islamic Republic has enforced hijab and extensive social controls since 1979. Economic stagnation, youth unemployment, ethnic discrimination and political repression created discontent. Women have long been at the front line of resistance.

Key Actors

Young women and schoolgirls have been the most visible force — removing hijabs, cutting hair in public and leading chants. University students, workers, bazaar merchants, artists and ethnic minorities (especially Kurds and Baluch) joined. The movement has remained largely leaderless and decentralized.

Scale, Character & Obstacles

  • Protests spread to all 31 provinces within weeks.
  • Public unveiling, burning of hijabs and direct confrontation with security forces became common.
  • The regime responded with mass arrests, internet blackouts, live fire and later executions. Hundreds of protesters were killed; thousands were detained.
  • Critical obstacles remain: the security apparatus (IRGC, Basij) has largely held, the movement lacks institutions capable of replacing the state, and open street protests declined under sustained repression.
The movement has already achieved a cultural shift: compulsory hijab enforcement has been visibly weakened in many urban areas and the slogan “Woman, Life, Freedom” has become a lasting symbol of resistance inside Iran and in the diaspora.

Lessons: Connecting 1917 to Today

Applying the success criteria developed from the Russian and French cases to the Iranian movement reveals both strengths and critical gaps.

Success Factor Russian 1917 Iran 2022–
Clear program for power Yes – soviet power + concrete decrees Partial – strong moral demand, weaker institutional plan
Organized force to seize institutions Yes – Red Guards + soviet majorities No – decentralized, no parallel institutions
Neutralization of security forces Yes – mass mutiny in Petrograd No – security forces largely held
Ability to survive counter-attack Yes – won the Civil War Unproven – repression has contained open revolt so far

Recommendations Drawn from Historical Precedent

1
Build institutions — Historical success required organizations that could coordinate action and later administer power (soviets, revolutionary committees). Purely spontaneous street protests are powerful but temporary without structures.
2
Target the loyalty of the apparatus — Both the French and Russian cases turned when significant parts of the army or police refused orders or switched sides. Strategies that raise the cost of repression for security personnel matter.
3
Link demands to concrete material ones — “Peace, Land and Bread” gave the Bolsheviks a program that spoke to soldiers and peasants. Connecting gender freedom to economic relief and political voice broadens the coalition.
4
Prepare for the day after — Revolutions that only destroy the old order without a plan for governance often produce chaos or new authoritarianism. Clear transitional proposals reduce fear among potential supporters inside the state.

Assessment of Trajectory

The Iranian movement has already changed the cultural landscape and demonstrated the regime’s deep legitimacy deficit. By the standards used here, however, it has not yet met the threshold of revolutionary success: sovereign power remains with the Islamic Republic, the security forces have not fractured, and no alternative institutional center has replaced the state.

History also shows that revolutions often fail in their first major wave and succeed only after multiple cycles of mobilization and learning. The Russian case itself required the 1905 Revolution before 1917 became possible. Continued pressure and the gradual erosion of regime cohesion could still shift the balance.
